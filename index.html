The Manifesto

We are uncovering better ways of developing software by changing the people, teams and reporting lines responsible for developing it.

Through this work we have come to value:

Reorganizations over solutions.
New reporting lines over clear responsibilities.
Organizational charts over architecture.
Moving people over removing blockers.
New managers over empowered teams.
Renaming teams over changing processes.
Cost reductions over continuity.

That is, while there is value in the things on the right, we value the things on the left more.

"The new structure will make us more efficient."
— The official outcome, effective immediately

The Principles

01

Every Problem Is an Organizational Problem

If something isn't working, the organizational structure must be responsible. Technical explanations are usually symptoms of insufficient alignment.

02

Never Waste a Good Crisis

A production incident, missed deadline or difficult delivery is an excellent opportunity to announce a reorganization.

03

Ownership Is Temporary

No team should own the same responsibility long enough to become too comfortable being accountable for it.

04

Rename Before You Refactor

Changing a team's name creates the perception of progress without introducing unnecessary technical risk.

05

The People Are the Architecture

Microservices can be consolidated. Teams can be split. Managers can be reassigned. Everything is flexible except the delivery deadline.

06

Every Reorganization Creates Opportunities

Especially opportunities to explain why previous commitments, priorities and delivery dates are no longer valid.

07

Institutional Memory Is Technical Debt

A new organizational structure deserves a fresh start. Previous decisions belong to the previous organization.

08

Alignment Is a Deliverable

When delivery becomes difficult, schedule alignment meetings. Progress can wait until everyone is aligned on what progress means.

09

Repeat Until Success Is Declared

Eventually, the organization will stabilize. Or people will stop asking.

The Economics of RDD

Reorganizations are one of the most effective ways to reduce operational costs. At least, according to the spreadsheet prepared before the reorganization.

Removing positions immediately reduces costs. Moving people between teams creates synergies. Reassigning responsibilities eliminates duplication.

The productivity dip during the transition is an implementation detail. Ramp-up time is not a cost. It is an investment in organizational agility.

"The savings are immediate. The benefits will follow."
— The business case

Cost Reduction Through Reorganization

Reduce management overhead by creating new management positions. Increase efficiency by distributing experienced people across unfamiliar teams. Reduce headcount costs while increasing the workload of everyone who remains.

Productivity Is an Accounting Choice

Lost productivity is temporary. Reduced headcount is permanent. Therefore, the savings are immediate, while the productivity impact is merely theoretical.

The Reorganization ROI Principle

The financial benefits of a reorganization should be measured immediately. Its operational consequences should be evaluated after the next reorganization.

What is Reorg Driven Development?

Reorg Driven Development is an approach to software development in which organizational change is the primary mechanism for solving technical and operational problems.

reorg(problem) { movePeople(); renameTeams(); updateReportingLines(); declareSynergies(); }

Unlike conventional problem solving, RDD focuses on changing the structure around a problem rather than necessarily addressing the problem itself.

The new organization chart provides a clear direction. The details can be worked out during the transition.

The Reality

Organizations change for legitimate reasons: new business priorities, acquisitions, cost pressures, changing markets, duplicated responsibilities and the need to adapt.

Sometimes reorganizations are necessary. Sometimes they improve collaboration, clarify ownership and reduce costs.

But organizational change is not free.

People need time to understand new responsibilities, rebuild working relationships, transfer knowledge and learn how decisions are made. During that time, delivery can slow down and institutional knowledge can disappear.

"The organization chart changed overnight. The organization itself will take a little longer."

The problem starts when the cost of the transition is ignored, the expected benefits are assumed, and another reorganization arrives before the previous one has had time to work.

The Laws of RDD

Law #1 — The Reorganization Conservation Law

The number of organizational changes is inversely proportional to the amount of actual change delivered.

Law #2 — The Accountability Migration Law

Every reorganization moves accountability one level further away from the people doing the work.

Law #3 — The Reset Law

Every new organizational structure resets the delivery expectations clock.

Law #4 — The Naming Law

A sufficiently creative team name can compensate for the absence of a clear mission.

Law #5 — The Ramp-up Exemption

Productivity lost during a reorganization does not count toward its cost if nobody includes it in the business case.

Law #6 — The Synergy Principle

Any two teams placed under the same manager are immediately considered synergistic, regardless of what they actually do.

Unfortunately, This Is Real

RDD is satire. The behavior it describes is not.

Organizational changes can be necessary and beneficial. But reorganizations also have real costs: lost context, disrupted relationships, unclear ownership, duplicated work and the time required for people to become effective again.

Those costs rarely appear on the slide announcing the savings.

Reduce costs today.
Let someone else deal with the consequences tomorrow.

Reorg Driven Development is an informal and satirical concept. It is not an established software development methodology, framework, certification or professional standard (officially).
